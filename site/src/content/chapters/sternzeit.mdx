A sky renderer that wants to look like the real sky first has to know what the real sky looks like right now. Where is
the Sun? How high is the Moon, how large does it appear, which way is its crescent tilted? Is there an eclipse going
on? None of this is guesswork: astronomers have been computing it for centuries, and the formulas are well documented.
They are just scattered across books written for telescope operators rather than graphics programmers.

This chapter walks through that math, from how astronomers count time to why the Moon seems to wobble. Everything
below is computed live, in your browser, by the library itself: a TypeScript port of the astronomy core of osgHimmel,
a C++ sky renderer written for a master's thesis in 2012 <Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />.

## Counting time the astronomer's way

Calendars are a mess. Months have different lengths, leap years come and go, and in 1582 ten days were simply skipped.
None of that belongs in orbital mechanics, so astronomers count days instead: the <Term of="Julian Day" /> is the
number of days since noon on January 1st, 4713 BCE. It is a single, continuous number, fractional for the time of day.
Today is somewhere around 2.46 million.

Most formulas then don't use the day count directly but <Term of="Julian century">Julian centuries</Term> since
<Term of="J2000.0">J2000</Term>, noon on January 1st, 2000. Orbital motion drifts slowly, and polynomials in centuries
keep the coefficients in a numerically comfortable range.

Then there is <Term of="Sidereal time">sidereal time</Term>. A solar day, noon to noon, is 24 hours. But Earth also
moves along its orbit, so it has to turn a little more than once for the Sun to come back to the same spot. Relative
to the distant stars, one rotation takes about 23 hours and 56 minutes. Sidereal time measures that rotation, as an
angle. It is what connects the sky's coordinates to your spot on the ground: in the scene, the dashed meridian through
your location rotates with it.

```ts
import { julianDayUT, siderealTime } from "@himmel/sternzeit";

// 2026-09-21, 20:00 in Berlin (UTC+2)
const time = {
    year: 2026, month: 9, day: 21,
    hour: 20, minute: 0, second: 0, utcOffsetSeconds: 7200,
};
const jd = julianDayUT(time); // 2461305.25, the offset is applied
const theta = siderealTime(time); // Greenwich mean sidereal time, in degrees
```

## Earth, the platform we observe from

Earth's rotation axis is tilted against the plane of its orbit by about 23.4°, the
<Term of="Obliquity of the ecliptic">obliquity of the ecliptic</Term>, and that is why we have seasons. The tilt is not
constant. It changes slowly over millennia (the _mean_ obliquity), and on top of that the Moon's pull on Earth's
equatorial bulge makes the axis nod with a period of 18.6 years, a wobble called <Term of="Nutation">nutation</Term>.
It has two components: one in obliquity, tilting the axis a little more and a little less, and one in longitude,
sliding the point from which the sky's coordinates are measured, the vernal equinox. Both are tiny, below 20
arcseconds, but they separate a position that is almost right from one that is right.

In the scene, the true obliquity is the arc between Earth's rotation axis and the dotted axis of its orbit. The orbit
itself is the dashed ellipse around everything, and at an eccentricity of about 0.0167 <Cite id="bretagnon1982" /> it
is honestly almost a circle.

## The Sun

Computing the Sun's position follows a pattern that most of this library repeats. Start from where the body _would_ be
on a perfectly circular orbit traversed at constant speed: its <Term of="Mean anomaly">mean anomaly</Term> and
<Term of="Mean longitude">mean longitude</Term>, both almost linear in time. Then correct for reality. Orbits are
ellipses, so add the <Term of="Equation of the center">equation of the center</Term> to get the
<Term of="True longitude">true longitude</Term>. Correct that for nutation and for aberration, a small shift caused by
Earth's own orbital speed, to get the <Term of="Apparent position">apparent position</Term> in
<Term of="Equatorial coordinates">right ascension and declination</Term>, the sky's own latitude and longitude.

That position is still geocentric, as seen from Earth's center. For an actual observer on the surface, the
<Term of="Parallax">topocentric position</Term> corrects for where they stand, and the
<Term of="Horizontal coordinates">horizontal position</Term> turns it into what a renderer wants: an altitude above the
horizon and an azimuth along it, measured from north through east.

## The Moon

The Moon is the hard one. It is close, its orbit is tilted against Earth's by about 5°, and the Sun actually pulls on
it about twice as hard as Earth does. Its position needs dozens of periodic correction terms, built from four
fundamental angles: its <Term of="Mean anomaly">mean anomaly</Term>, its
<Term of="Mean elongation">mean elongation</Term> from the Sun, its
<Term of="Argument of latitude">mean argument of latitude</Term>, and the longitude of its
<Term of="Longitude of the ascending node">ascending node</Term>, where its orbit crosses Earth's heading north.

Being close also means <Term of="Parallax">parallax</Term>: depending on where on Earth you stand, the Moon appears
shifted by up to a degree, about two of its own diameters. That is why the topocentric correction matters most here.

The last rows are for rendering the Moon rather than locating it. The Moon always shows the same face, almost: tilted

<Term of="Libration">optical libration</Term>. The <Term of="Position angle of axis">position angle of the axis</Term>
says how the Moon's north pole is oriented on the sky, and the <Term of="Parallactic angle">parallactic angle</Term>
how that sky direction relates to your local "up". Together they determine how the Moon's surface, and its lit
crescent, appear rotated for you.

## Eclipses

With both bodies located, eclipses come almost for free. A solar eclipse is a matter of perspective: the Moon passing
in front of the Sun, which depends on where you stand. A lunar eclipse is the Moon passing through Earth's shadow, and
it looks the same from anywhere the Moon is up. Both are summarized as a single
<Term of="Eclipse phase">phase</Term> <Cite id="mueller2012vmv" />: 0 at the deepest point, 0.5 where a total or
annular eclipse turns partial, 1 where the discs, or the Moon and the shadow, just touch, and above 1 when there is
nothing to see. The fixed 0.5 boundary is what makes the phase usable as a texture coordinate for a shader.

## Precise or approximate?

Every quantity comes in two variants. The **precise** one follows Jean Meeus' _Astronomical Algorithms_
<Cite id="meeus1998" />, the standard reference for this kind of computation. The **approximate** one follows the
night sky model of Jensen et al. <Cite id="jensen2001" />, with far fewer terms. Both columns sit side by side in the
tables; hover an approximate value to see how far off it is.

For most rendering, the approximation is plenty. Over a year, the Sun's approximate altitude stays within 0.004° of the
precise one, invisible next to a disc half a degree wide. The Moon's can be off by almost a tenth of a degree, a fifth
of its diameter: fine for a sky dome, not for an eclipse. That is what the precise variant is for, along with the exact
moment of sunset or a telescope view. Both live behind the same names, so switching is a change of import path.

```ts
import { sun } from "@himmel/sternzeit"; // precise
import { sun as sunApprox } from "@himmel/sternzeit/approx"; // approximate
```

## Using it

`@himmel/sternzeit` has no dependencies, touches neither the DOM nor the GPU, and runs anywhere JavaScript runs. It
returns plain numbers, and it is up to you what to do with them. For example, the direction vector that
[dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) takes to light its atmosphere:

```ts
import { DEG_TO_RAD, fromJulianDay, sun } from "@himmel/sternzeit";

const { altitude, azimuth } = sun.horizontalPosition(fromJulianDay(jd), latitude, longitude);
const a = altitude * DEG_TO_RAD;
const z = azimuth * DEG_TO_RAD;
const sunDirection = [Math.cos(a) * Math.cos(z), Math.cos(a) * Math.sin(z), Math.sin(a)]; // x north, y east, z up
```
