A sky renderer that wants to look like the real sky first has to know what the real sky looks like right now. Where is
the Sun? How high is the Moon, how large does it appear, which way is its crescent tilted? Is there an eclipse going
on? None of this is guesswork: astronomers have been computing it for centuries, and the formulas are well documented.
They are just scattered across books written for telescope operators rather than graphics programmers.

This chapter does not derive those formulas; the references do. It goes through what the numbers mean, how they fit
together and what to do with them, from how astronomers count time to why the Moon seems to wobble. Everything below
is computed live, in your browser, by the library itself, the astronomy core of osgHimmel rebuilt in TypeScript
<Cite id="mueller2012thesis" />.

<Scene />

The discs are drawn from the Sun's and the Moon's actual apparent diameters, and they almost match: the Sun is about
400 times wider than the Moon and about 400 times farther away. That is pure luck, and it will not last. The Moon
drifts away from Earth by about 3.8 cm a year, and in some 600 million years it will be too small to ever cover the Sun
again, leaving only annular eclipses. We happen to live in the stretch of Earth's history in which the sky can go dark
in the middle of the day.

## Counting time the astronomer's way

Calendars are a mess. Months have different lengths, leap years come and go, and in 1582 ten days were simply skipped.
None of that belongs in orbital mechanics, so astronomers count days instead: the <Term of="Julian Day" /> is the
number of days since noon on January 1st, 4713 BCE. It is a single, continuous number, fractional for the time of day.
Today is somewhere around 2.46 million.

Most formulas then don't use the day count directly but <Term of="Julian century">Julian centuries</Term> since
<Term of="J2000.0">J2000</Term>, noon on January 1st, 2000. Orbital motion drifts slowly, and polynomials in centuries
keep the coefficients in a numerically comfortable range.

Then there is <Term of="Sidereal time">sidereal time</Term>. A solar day, noon to noon, is 24 hours. But Earth also
moves along its orbit, so it has to turn a little more than once for the Sun to come back to the same spot. Relative
to the distant stars, one rotation takes about 23 hours and 56 minutes. Sidereal time measures that rotation, as an
angle. It is what connects the sky's coordinates to your spot on the ground: in the [armillary sphere](#armillary-sphere), the dashed meridian through
your location rotates with it.

```ts
import {
    fromDate, fromJulianDay, julianDayUT, siderealTime, toDate,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Any JavaScript Date, e.g. new Date() for now; its time zone
// offset comes along.
const time = fromDate(new Date("2026-09-21T20:00:00+02:00"));
// The same moment spelled out: 20:00 in Berlin (UTC+2).
const same = {
    year: 2026, month: 9, day: 21,
    hour: 20, minute: 0, second: 0, utcOffsetSeconds: 7200,
};

const jd = julianDayUT(time); // 2461305.25, the offset applied
const theta = siderealTime(time); // Greenwich mean sidereal, in deg
const date = toDate(fromJulianDay(jd)); // and back to a Date
```

## Earth, the platform we observe from

Earth's rotation axis is tilted against the plane of its orbit by about 23.4°, the
<Term of="Obliquity of the ecliptic">obliquity of the ecliptic</Term>, and that is why we have seasons. The tilt is not
constant. It changes slowly over millennia (the _mean_ obliquity), and on top of that the Moon's pull on Earth's
equatorial bulge makes the axis nod with a period of 18.6 years, a wobble called <Term of="Nutation">nutation</Term>.
It has two components: one in obliquity, tilting the axis a little more and a little less, and one in longitude,
sliding the point from which the sky's coordinates are measured, the vernal equinox. Both are tiny, below 20
arcseconds, but they separate a position that is almost right from one that is right.

In the [armillary sphere](#armillary-sphere), the true obliquity is the arc between Earth's rotation axis and the dotted axis of its orbit. The orbit
itself is the dashed ellipse around everything, and at an eccentricity of about 0.0167 <Cite id="bretagnon1982" /> it
is honestly almost a circle.

The table also holds the constants the atmosphere needs: Earth's radius <Cite id="nssdc" />, the thickness of the
atmosphere <Cite id="nishita1993" /> <Cite id="bruneton2008" />, and the
<Term of="Atmospheric refraction">refraction</Term> that makes the Sun linger visibly above the horizon for a few
minutes after it has geometrically set. Refraction comes in two directions, from the true altitude
<Cite id="saemundsson1986" /> and from the apparent one <Cite id="bennett1982" />, and a renderer bending its view rays
wants the latter. Both rows, like the distance a ray travels through the atmosphere, are evaluated towards the Sun.
Once the Sun is more than a degree below the horizon, refraction no longer means anything and shows as n/a, while
the ray towards it simply ends in the ground: 0 km from sea level, a little more from a mountain, whose horizon lies
lower.

## The Sun

Computing the Sun's position follows a pattern that most of this library repeats. Start from where the body _would_ be
on a perfectly circular orbit traversed at constant speed: its <Term of="Mean anomaly">mean anomaly</Term> and
<Term of="Mean longitude">mean longitude</Term>, both almost linear in time. Then correct for reality. Orbits are
ellipses, so add the <Term of="Equation of the center">equation of the center</Term> to get the
<Term of="True longitude">true longitude</Term>. Correct that for nutation and for aberration, a small shift caused by
Earth's own orbital speed, to get the <Term of="Apparent position">apparent position</Term> in
<Term of="Equatorial coordinates">right ascension and declination</Term>, the sky's own latitude and longitude.

That position is still geocentric, as seen from Earth's center. For an actual observer on the surface, the
<Term of="Parallax">topocentric position</Term> corrects for where they stand, and the
<Term of="Horizontal coordinates">horizontal position</Term> turns it into what a renderer wants: an altitude above the
horizon and an azimuth along it, measured from north through east.

Step through a year and watch the Sun's arc rise and sink with the seasons. The Moon moves about 13° a day against the

The analemma's height swings with the seasons, from the tilt of Earth's axis. Its sideways swing comes
from the <Term of="Equation of time">equation of time</Term>: Earth moves faster along its orbit in January than in
July, and the tilt adds a second, twice-yearly wobble, so the Sun runs up to about 16 minutes ahead of the clock or
behind it <Cite id="meeus1998" />. Where its dots crowd, the Sun is turning around.

## The Moon

The Moon is the hard one. It is close, its orbit is tilted against Earth's by about 5°, and the Sun actually pulls on
it about twice as hard as Earth does. Its position needs dozens of periodic correction terms, built from four
fundamental angles: its <Term of="Mean anomaly">mean anomaly</Term>, its
<Term of="Mean elongation">mean elongation</Term> from the Sun, its
<Term of="Argument of latitude">mean argument of latitude</Term>, and the longitude of its
<Term of="Longitude of the ascending node">ascending node</Term>, where its orbit crosses Earth's heading north.

Being close also means <Term of="Parallax">parallax</Term>: depending on where on Earth you stand, the Moon appears
shifted by up to a degree, about two of its own diameters. That is why the topocentric correction matters most here.

<Controls />

The rows after `distance` are for rendering the Moon rather than locating it. The Moon always shows the same face, almost: tilted
orbit and varying speed let us peek a few degrees around its edges over a month, the
<Term of="Libration">optical libration</Term>. The <Term of="Position angle of axis">position angle of the axis</Term>
says how the Moon's north pole is oriented on the sky, and the <Term of="Parallactic angle">parallactic angle</Term>
how that sky direction relates to your local "up". Together they determine how the Moon's surface, and its lit
crescent, appear rotated for you.

The last four rows light it. The <Term of="Phase angle">phase angle</Term>, the angle Sun-Moon-Earth, says how much of
the lit half faces us, and the <Term of="Illuminated fraction">illuminated fraction</Term> turns that into what the
phase looks like, from 0 at new moon to 1 at full moon <Cite id="meeus1998" />. For actual shading, a renderer wants
the light direction itself: `sunDirection` points from the Moon's center to the Sun, in the same frame as every other
direction here: the observer's local east, north and up, a right-handed frame known as ENU. It is not quite the Sun's direction as seen from Earth, since the Moon sits a few hundred thousand
kilometers off to the side, but it is close.

The night side is not entirely dark either. The Earth reflects sunlight too, and around new moon, when the Moon sees
an almost full Earth, that <Term of="Earthshine">earthshine</Term> gives the unlit part of a thin crescent a faint,
ashen glow. How strong it is follows from the Earth's phase as seen from the Moon <Cite id="vandehulst1980" />, scaled
by the Earth's albedo <Cite id="jensen2001" />.

## Eclipses

With both bodies located, eclipses come almost for free. A solar eclipse is a matter of perspective: the Moon passing
in front of the Sun, which depends on where you stand. Stand on the line the Moon's shadow sweeps and the Sun goes out
(_total_), or, with the Moon near apogee and too small to cover it, leaves a ring of light around the black disc
(_annular_); a few hundred kilometers to the side the Moon only takes a bite out of it (_partial_). A lunar eclipse is
the Moon passing through Earth's shadow, and it looks the same from anywhere the Moon is up: _total_ once the whole
disc is inside the umbra, _partial_ while only part of it is, and _penumbral_, barely a dimming, when the Moon misses
the umbra altogether. Both are summarized as a single
<Term of="Eclipse phase">phase</Term> <Cite id="mueller2012vmv" />: 0 at the deepest point, 0.5 where a total or
annular eclipse turns partial, 1 where the discs, or the Moon and the shadow, just touch, and above 1 when there is
nothing to see. The fixed 0.5 boundary is what makes the phase usable as a texture coordinate for a shader.

<Eclipse />
<Controls />

Most of the time there is nothing to see, hence the four buttons: they search the sky itself, forwards or backwards
from the moment you are on, for the next eclipse, and jump to its maximum. Nothing is looked up, there is no table of
dates anywhere on this page. Only a full moon can enter Earth's shadow and only a new moon can cross the Sun, so the
search steps from one lunation to the next, 29.53 days apart, instead of walking the days in between; a handful of
calls per candidate rules out the ones where the two bodies stay far apart, and what is left is walked minute by
minute. Twenty years either way take about twenty milliseconds. The lunar search answers for the whole night side of
Earth at once. The solar one answers for the place you chose, which is why it often lands on a modest partial
eclipse, and why it sometimes has to look years ahead: the same eclipse that turns day into night along its central
line does no more than take a bite out of the Sun a thousand kilometers away. From the maximum, step the Julian Day by minutes and
watch it unfold. The Moon
darkens in the penumbra first, barely noticeable, then turns a dark red in the umbra, lit only by sunlight that Earth's
atmosphere bends around the planet, its outer rim tinged blue by the ozone the light passes through. The panel only
hints at those colors; rendering them properly, along with the Moon's surface, is the job of
[nachtgestirn](../nachtgestirn/).

## Precise or approximate?

Every quantity comes in two variants. The **precise** one follows Jean Meeus' _Astronomical Algorithms_
<Cite id="meeus1998" />, the standard reference for this kind of computation. The **approximate** one follows the
night sky model of Jensen et al. <Cite id="jensen2001" />, with far fewer terms. Both columns sit side by side in the
tables; hover an approximate value to see how far off it is.

For most rendering, the approximation is plenty. Over a year, the Sun's approximate altitude stays within 0.004° of the
precise one, invisible next to a disc half a degree wide. The Moon's can be off by almost a tenth of a degree, a fifth
of its diameter: fine for a sky dome, not for an eclipse. That is what the precise variant is for, along with the exact
moment of sunset or a telescope view. Both live behind the same names, so switching is a change of import path.

```ts
import { sun } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit"; // precise
import { sun as fast } from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit/approx"; // approximate
```

## Code examples

`@himmelszelt/sternzeit` has no dependencies, touches neither the DOM nor the GPU, and runs anywhere JavaScript runs. It
returns plain numbers, and it is up to you what to do with them. A CAD or architecture viewer with the correct
light and shadows for a real site and hour. A sun study for a solar roof. A game whose day follows the player's real
clock. A photo planner that knows when the full Moon rises behind a landmark. A moon calendar. An augmented reality
overlay that labels what is up there right now. A sundial, or the sky over a date in history. Here are four more
ideas to start with, code included:

**Light a scene.** The two directions a renderer wants, the ones
[dunstkreis](../dunstkreis/) takes to light its atmosphere, are one call each, and the Moon's brightness comes with
them: moonlight is sunlight, so the lit fraction of the disc is roughly how much of it there is.

```ts
import {
    fromJulianDay, horizontalToDirection, moon, sun,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const time = fromJulianDay(jd);
// Unit vectors in the observer's ENU frame: x east, y north, z up.
// A y-up engine takes them as (x, z, -y).
const toSun = horizontalToDirection(
    sun.horizontalPosition(time, latitude, longitude),
);
const toMoon = horizontalToDirection(
    moon.horizontalPosition(time, latitude, longitude),
);
const moonlight = moon.illuminatedFraction(jd); // 0 new, 1 full
```

Shading the Moon itself is a different question, and an optional one: the light hitting it does not come from where
the observer stands but from 384,000 km away, which `moon.sunDirection` accounts for. That vector, with the libration
and the earthshine, is what [nachtgestirn](../nachtgestirn/) will want.

**Find the golden hour.** There is no `sunrise()` function, and there does not need to be: sunrise is where the Sun's
altitude crosses the horizon, so walking a day in one-minute steps finds it, and the same loop finds dusk, the blue
hour or any other threshold you care to render.

```ts
import {
    fromDate, fromJulianDay, julianDayUT, sun, toDate,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// The Sun is up once its refracted upper limb clears the horizon,
// the -0.833 degrees every almanac uses.
const midnight = Math.floor(julianDayUT(fromDate(new Date())) - 0.5) + 0.5;
const above = (jd: number, deg: number) =>
    sun.horizontalPosition(fromJulianDay(jd), latitude, longitude)
        .altitude > deg;

function crossings(deg: number): Date[] {
    const found: Date[] = [];
    for (let minute = 1; minute <= 1440; minute++) {
        const [before, now] = [minute - 1, minute]
            .map((m) => midnight + m / 1440);
        if (above(before, deg) !== above(now, deg)) {
            found.push(toDate(fromJulianDay(now)));
        }
    }
    return found;
}

const [sunrise, sunset] = crossings(-0.833);
const [dawn, dusk] = crossings(-6); // civil twilight
```

**Draw the Moon the right way round.** A phase widget that only scales a crescent gets the tilt wrong: the lit side
points at the Sun, which is rarely straight down. The position angle from the Moon to the Sun is the angle to rotate
the crescent by, measured on the sky from north through east.

```ts
import {
    fromDate, julianDayUT, moon, positionAngle, sun,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

const jd = julianDayUT(fromDate(new Date()));
const lit = moon.illuminatedFraction(jd); // 0 new, 1 full
// Half a day on tells waxing from waning without a calendar.
const waxing = moon.illuminatedFraction(jd + 0.5) > lit;

const m = moon.apparentPosition(jd);
const s = sun.apparentPosition(jd);
const brightLimb = positionAngle(
    m.rightAscension, m.declination,
    s.rightAscension, s.declination,
);
```

That angle is worth taking seriously, because getting it wrong is a classic. Games and illustrations like to hang a
crescent in the night sky with its lit side facing up, and that cannot happen. The lit side faces the Sun, and a
crescent means the Sun is less than 90° from the Moon, so pointing the lit side up puts the Sun above the Moon and
therefore above the horizon: it would be day. A gibbous or full Moon is a different matter. There the Sun stands on
the far side of the sky, and the lit side may well face up while the Sun is 80° below the horizon. For a crescent,
point the lit side at least somewhat towards the horizon and you are right without computing anything.

The other classic is the unlit part. It is not a hole: no star has ever shone through the Moon. What is faintly there
instead is earthshine, sunlight that went to Earth and back, and it is one call away (`moon.earthshine`), which is how
<Fig id="moon-view" /> above shades the Moon's night side.

**Know when to bother.** Eclipses are rare enough that a renderer should not compute them every frame, and special
enough to be worth a notification when one comes. A lunar eclipse is pure geometry, the same from every place on the
night side, so a scan ahead finds the next one, no ephemeris table needed. This is, give or take the search window,
what the buttons under <Fig id="eclipse-views" /> above do:

```ts
import {
    eclipse, fromDate, fromJulianDay, julianDayUT, moon, toDate,
} from "@himmelszelt/sternzeit";

// Only a full moon can be eclipsed, so step by lunations rather than
// by days. Full moons sit half a lunation after the counted new ones.
const { MEAN_NEW_MOON, MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH } = moon;
const firstFullMoon = MEAN_NEW_MOON + MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH / 2;

// Totality: the whole disc fits inside the umbra.
const total = (jd: number) => {
    const shadow = eclipse.lunar(jd);
    return shadow.axisOffsetKm + moon.MEAN_RADIUS_KM
        <= shadow.umbraRadiusKm;
};

function nextTotalLunarEclipse(from: number): Date | null {
    const gone = (from - firstFullMoon) / MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH;
    for (let month = Math.floor(gone); month < gone + 250; month++) {
        const full = firstFullMoon + month * MEAN_SYNODIC_MONTH;
        // The true full moon wanders up to a day from the mean one;
        // three-hour steps say whether to bother looking closer.
        let near = false;
        for (let jd = full - 1; jd <= full + 1 && !near; jd += 1 / 8) {
            const shadow = eclipse.lunar(jd);
            near = shadow.axisOffsetKm < 2 * shadow.penumbraRadiusKm;
        }
        if (!near) continue;
        for (let jd = full - 1; jd <= full + 1; jd += 1 / 1440) {
            if (jd > from && total(jd)) return toDate(fromJulianDay(jd));
        }
    }
    return null;
}

// Up to twenty years ahead in a twentieth of a second; from late 2026
// that is totality on 2028-12-31, from 16:19 UTC.
const next = nextTotalLunarEclipse(julianDayUT(fromDate(new Date())));
```

The same goes for the other libraries: the Moon's phase, libration, sun direction and earthshine are what
[nachtgestirn](../nachtgestirn/) shades the Moon with, sidereal time is what will turn the stars of
[sternenheer](../sternenheer/), and the Sun's and the Moon's directions light the clouds of [gewoelk](../gewoelk/).
